HIMSHAKTI / KAVACH — SIH 2026 Project Plan

PS 26061 · AI-Driven Smart Energy Management System for Polar Research Stations · Ministry of Earth Sciences (MoES) / NCPOR · Software · Clean & Green Technology

1. Context & framing

The PS text is deliberately thin, so the framing is ours to choose — and the framing decides whether we win.

Our framing: NCPOR's stations (Maitri, Bharati in Antarctica; Himadri in the Arctic) are electrical islands resupplied with diesel once a year, by ship. That single fact dominates everything. Fuel is not a cost line — it is the survival margin. So we are not building a green dashboard. We are building a fuel-survival optimizer, with a days-of-autonomy countdown as the hero metric.

The technical insight the whole pitch rests on

A diesel genset at 25 % load burns roughly 1.5–2× more fuel per kWh than at 75 %, and below ~30 % it wet-stacks and damages itself. But you cannot simply switch it off, because its waste heat is what heats the buildings and melts snow for water. Electricity and heat are coupled — optimise them separately and you get the wrong answer. Co-optimising them is the core of our solution, and the thing competing teams will miss.

Locked decisions

  • Station modelled: Bharati — year-round crew, modern, best documented.
  • Centrepiece: fuel-survival optimizer. Engine: rule-based + MILP hybrid.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop: optional stretch goal only, after software is locked.
  • Stage: internal hackathon + idea PPT first, built to extend to the December Grand Finale without rework. SPOC has approved; dates pending.
  • Product name: team to choose — HIMSHAKTI (हिम snow + शक्ति power) or KAVACH (कवच armour/shield).

2. Why the optimizer, and not the other three options

This is a decision about what leads the pitch and where the depth goes — not about what we cut. Forecasting and blizzard-response are both still built. They just aren't the headline.

Why not forecasting as the centrepiece?

A forecast answers "what will happen." It never answers "so what do we do about it." The first question any judge asks is "and then?" — if the answer is a chart, we've lost. Worse, forecasting is the commodity choice: most teams picking this PS will build exactly this, putting us in a comparison on MAPE, a number NCPOR does not care about. We get forecasting anyway as Layer 2. We just don't lead with it.

Why not blizzard / crisis resilience?

Dramatic, but narrow. A blizzard is an edge case lasting a few days a year; NCPOR's actual problem is 365 days of fuel discipline. It is also hard to quantify — "the crew survived" is not a number that fits on a slide, and unquantified claims lose to measured ones. In our design this is a scenario button, not a product: we still get the drama on stage, we just don't build the company around it.

Why not all of them, equally weighted?

This is the classic way strong teams lose. A SIH pitch is a few minutes long — split evenly across four capabilities, each gets about a minute, and nothing is memorable. Judges retain one number. Equal weighting also splits build time into four mediocre modules instead of one excellent one with three solid supports. Against domain experts, depth beats breadth every time.

Why the fuel-survival optimizer wins

  1. It is the only option producing a single defensible number tied to the mission — extra days of autonomy. Everything else produces charts.
  2. It is where EEE knowledge is a moat: genset efficiency curves, minimum stable load, N-1 reserve, waste-heat coupling. A CS-heavy team cannot easily reach this, and MoES judges will recognise it.
  3. It addresses the PS's own third clause — "fuel optimization" — which is also NCPOR's real operational pain, given one resupply ship a year.
  4. It subsumes the others. Forecasting becomes its input; blizzard response becomes a scenario it handles. Choosing it loses us nothing and focuses everything.

3. Architecture — five layers

[L1 PolarSim] physics-based digital twin → synthetic year of station data ↓ [L2 Forecast] 24–72 h ahead: elec load, heat load, solar, wind ↓ [L3 Optimizer] MILP rolling-horizon dispatch (+ rule-based fallback) ← THE HERO ↓ [L4 Fuel Ledger] annual budget, days-of-autonomy, stockout risk ↓ [L5 Dashboard] live sim + scenario playground + baseline-vs-AI comparison

L1 — PolarSim (digital twin)

Why it exists: there is no public NCPOR energy dataset — nobody at SIH will have real telemetry. So we write a physics-grounded simulator, calibrate it against published Antarctic station energy studies, and state this openly in the PPT as a methodology choice, not an apology. It also lets us demo a blizzard on stage, which real data never would. Generates 1 year at 15-min resolution:

  • Weather — temperature, windspeed, solar elevation with true polar-night / midnight-sun cycles; blizzards as correlated wind/temperature excursions.
  • Building thermal — heat loss Q = U·A·ΔT plus ventilation and infiltration; snow-melt water demand vs crew size.
  • Occupancy — summer campaign (~50–60) vs winter-over (~20–25). Biggest single load driver.
  • Load library — every load tagged critical / deferrable / sheddable, with a power profile and, for deferrables, a completion deadline.
  • Assets — 2–3 gensets with non-linear fuel curves (L/h vs load factor), waste-heat recovery, backup boiler, cold-derated battery, PV with snow soiling, wind turbine with cut-in/cut-out and icing lockout.
  • Event injection — blizzard(days), genset_failure(id), resupply_delay(days), crew_surge(n).

L2 — Forecast

  • Targets: electrical load, thermal load, PV availability, wind availability. Horizon 72 h, hourly.
  • LightGBM on lag features + rolling stats + weather + occupancy + calendar. Small, trains in seconds, runs on station hardware with no GPU — say this, it's a credibility point.
  • Always report against a naive baseline (persistence / seasonal-naive). The lift is the story, not the absolute number.
  • Emit P10/P50/P90 quantiles, not a point forecast — the optimizer needs the uncertainty to size reserve.

L3 — Optimizer (build this before the forecaster)

Rolling-horizon MILP, 48 h lookahead, 1 h steps, re-solved each step. OR-Tools CP-SAT or PuLP/CBC.

  • Decisions: genset on/off (binary) and loading per unit; battery charge/discharge; deferrable load start times; boiler firing; renewable curtailment.
  • Objective: minimise litres burned, plus penalties for genset starts (wear), unmet thermal comfort, and unserved critical load (effectively infinite).
  • Constraints that carry the pitch: electrical balance; thermal balance including recovered waste heat (the coupling — our differentiator); genset minimum stable load (anti wet-stacking) and min up/down times; battery SoC with cold-temperature derating; deferrable deadlines; N-1 spinning reserve (survive the largest genset tripping).
  • Rule-based fallback: a deterministic priority dispatcher that always returns a feasible answer. If the solver stalls on stage, the demo continues. Not a hack — a life-safety system genuinely needs a deterministic path, so we present it as a design virtue.
  • Baseline for comparison: "always-on largest genset, no load shifting" — what stations realistically do today. Every saving we claim is measured against this.

L4 — Fuel Ledger  ·  L5 — Dashboard

  • Annual fuel burndown against the next resupply date; days-of-autonomy as the hero number; Monte Carlo over weather and occupancy → probability of stockout before resupply.
  • What-if: "ship delayed 21 days — do we survive?" Answered with a number, in front of the judges.
  • Dashboard hero panel: days-of-autonomy gauge + AI-vs-baseline fuel curves diverging over the year. Then accelerated sim clock, stacked dispatch chart (genset / PV / wind / battery), forecast-vs-actual.
  • Scenario buttons: Blizzard · Genset Failure · Resupply Delayed · Crew Surge.
  • Money slide, on screen: litres saved, % reduction, extra days of autonomy gained, CO₂ avoided.

Offline-first is a required talking point. Stations have thin, expensive satellite links. Everything runs on-prem on modest hardware — no cloud, no GPU. Say it explicitly; MoES judges will care.

5. Build phases

Phase 0 — Foundation (½ day). Repo scaffold, station-profile config schema, shared timestep data model.
Phase 1 — PolarSim (1½ days). Weather, thermal, occupancy, load library, assets, event injection. Ship a generated year as Parquet plus plots that look physically plausible. Gate: the annual profile must be defensible to an EEE professor.
Phase 2 — Optimizer + baseline (2 days) ← highest value, do it early. Rule-based dispatcher first, then MILP, then baseline, then the comparison harness. Initially run with perfect foresight (feed it the simulator's true future) so it can be built and validated before the forecaster exists. Gate: MILP beats baseline on fuel by a clear, reproducible margin.
Phase 3 — Forecast (1 day). Train models, swap them in behind the same interface perfect-foresight used. Measure how much of the saving survives real forecast error — report that honestly; it signals rigour, and judges probe for it.
Phase 4 — Dashboard (2 days). Hero panel, then dispatch charts, then scenario buttons. Do not start the UI until Phase 2 produces numbers worth displaying.
Phase 5 — Pitch (1 day). PPT in SIH format, a rehearsed 3-minute demo script, and a recorded fallback video in case the laptop or venue Wi-Fi fails.
Stretch (finale only). ESP32 + current sensors + relays driving a lamp/heater as a physical micro-grid, commanded by our controller. Powerful for an EEE team — but only after software is locked.

6. Idea PPT — section map

  1. Problem — one year of fuel, one ship, no second chance. Lead with the constraint, not the technology.
  2. Solution — the five layers, one diagram.
  3. Differentiator — heat/electricity coupling and genset low-load inefficiency. State plainly that electrical-only optimisation is wrong, and why.
  4. Technical approach — stack, MILP formulation sketch, forecast lift over baseline.
  5. Feasibility — offline, CPU-only, retrofits onto existing meters; deterministic fallback for life safety.
  6. Impact — litres saved → days of autonomy → reduced resupply risk → CO₂ and black-carbon reduction in a protected environment (Antarctic Treaty / Madrid Protocol framing).
  7. Research — published Antarctic station energy studies, NCPOR expedition reports, Maitri II's stated green-station goal. Show the simulator is calibrated, not invented.

7. Verification

  • Physical sanity — annual energy per capita and fuel burn land inside the range published for comparable Antarctic stations. If the sim says a 25-person station burns 20 L/day, it's wrong.
  • Energy balance — at every timestep, generation + discharge = load + charge + losses, within tolerance. Assert it in a test: a violated balance invalidates every saving we claim.
  • Optimizer correctness — on a small hand-checkable instance, MILP output matches a manually computed optimum. Confirm genset minimum-load and N-1 reserve are never violated across a full year.
  • The saving is real — same year, same weather seed, baseline vs AI; report litres and days gained across ≥10 random seeds so the headline isn't a lucky draw.
  • Forecast honesty — MAPE against persistence, and the saving measured under real forecast error, not perfect foresight.
  • Demo resilience — full run-through with Wi-Fi disabled; kill the solver mid-demo and confirm the rule-based fallback visibly takes over.
